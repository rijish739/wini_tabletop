How to Build the Classifier Step 1: Define the Cognitive Categories Based on your architecture, your classifier must detect the presence of the following signals: Question Answer attempt Explanation Confusion marker Misconception clue Transfer attempt Topic shift Step 2: Curate the Exemplar Bank Instead of training data, you need a diverse set of "exemplars" (anchor sentences) for each category. These should reflect actual, messy student language. Confusion Marker Exemplar: "I am totally lost on why the sign changed. "Transfer Attempt Exemplar: "Is this like when we calculated velocity in the last chapter? "Explanation Exemplar: "I think the bulb dims because the resistance is eating up the voltage. "Step 3: Generate MiniLM Embeddings Use a lightweight model like all-MiniLM-L6-v2. Pre-compute the vector embeddings for every exemplar in your bank and store them in memory or a vector database. Step 4: The Runtime Similarity EngineWhen a student sends a message: Generate the MiniLM embedding for the live utterance. Compute the Cosine Similarity between the student's embedding and all embeddings in the exemplar bank. Calculate a score for each category using a Top-K average (e.g., average the similarity scores of the top 3 matching exemplars in each category).Map these similarity scores to the required JSON output (e.g., "confusion": 0.72, "transfer_attempt": 0.41).2. What to Take Care Of (The "Dos")Implement Independent Thresholds: A student utterance can contain both a "confusion marker" and a "transfer attempt". Evaluate each category's score independently against its own threshold rather than comparing them to each other. Embrace "Messy" Language in Exemplars: Students rarely speak in perfect academic sentences. Your exemplars must include fragments, colloquialisms, and common filler words (e.g., "Wait, so...", "Umm, does that mean...", "I guess it's...").Chunk Compound Utterances: If a student writes, "I think it is because resistance is high, but why does current reduce?", consider splitting it by conjunctions or punctuation before embedding. Score the chunks separately and aggregate the highest signals to ensure the "explanation" (first half) and "question" (second half) are both captured. Normalize Context-Heavy Pronouns: If a student says "It goes up," MiniLM might struggle without context. Consider a lightweight preprocessing step that replaces "it" with the current active concept from the transient context cache.3. What NOT to Do (The "Don'ts")DO NOT Use Softmax or Mutually Exclusive Classes: This is the most critical rule. Softmax forces the model to pick one winning label, effectively reverting your system to the "Old architecture mindset". Always output continuous multi-label scores.DO NOT Strip "Stop Words": Traditional NLP often removes words like "but", "what if", "I", or "why". In cognitive state modeling, these words are the primary indicators of curiosity, hesitation, and load. Keep the text intact.DO NOT Bake Subject Matter into Exemplars: The Cognitive Input Processor should focus on the structure of thought, not the concept. Concept resolution happens in the next layer. Your exemplars should be subject-agnostic. Bad Exemplar: "Why does $E=mc^2$ mean mass is energy?" (Too specific).Good Exemplar: "Why does [Formula A] mean [Concept B] happens?"
